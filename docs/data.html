Process

  1. Collect

    We download the FCC's December 2025 availability files for New York, for cable, fiber and licensed fixed wireless. We also download NYC Planning's 2020 Census blocks and the City's franchise map.

  2. Match

    We count the buildings each company claims in every block and join the counts to block shapes by their 15-digit block ID. Each block takes the franchise area and the neighborhood that hold its center.

  3. Check

    We keep the companies that sell wired home service: Spectrum and Optimum cable, Astound, and Verizon fiber. Only claims of home service count, so a building where every claim is business-only drops out. Spectrum's and Optimum's own fiber counts with their cable. Wireless home internet stays in the block file and never counts as a wire.

  4. Calculate

    A block is one company's turf when only that company reports cable there, or when it reports at least four times the other's buildings. Otherwise both reach it. Buildings with only the cable company are at least the block's buildings less those Verizon and Astound claim. The two claims can overlap, so this is a minimum, and it never falls below zero. The map colors each block by its cable company and hatches it where neither Verizon nor Astound claims any of its buildings. The Other options view shades each block by that minimum, as a share of its buildings. A block with fewer than one building per two hectares is drawn as open land, with no company color or hatch. Those blocks are mostly parks, airports and cemeteries; they hold 0.2% of buildings and nearly a third of the land.

  5. Publish

    We simplify block shapes to about 2.5 meters and write the map file, the CSV and the summary.

Turf

Where one company holds a block, it is the company the City's franchise map names at 99.97% of buildings. Most of the 233 exceptions are in Marble Hill, which belongs to Manhattan but sits on the mainland beside the Bronx. The franchise map draws it with the Bronx, and Spectrum, Manhattan's franchisee, reports serving it.

About 1.1% of buildings sit on blocks both companies reach, and 82% of those are within 500 meters of the franchise line.

Fiber

When the City granted Verizon its citywide cable franchise in 2008, Verizon promised Fios to every household by 30 June 2014, under the franchise agreement. In the FCC's December 2025 data, Verizon claims fiber at 86.5% of the city's buildings and none at 109,400.

Reports cover the City's 2015 audit, its 2017 suit and the 2020 settlement. Verizon's position in the suit was that the dispute turned on what counts as a "passed" home.

Limits

  • A location is a building, so a tower with hundreds of apartments counts once, the same as a house. Queens and Staten Island hold more than half the city's buildings, so citywide shares lean toward houses.
  • Companies report the buildings where they say they could install service within 10 business days. The FCC doesn't check each claim, and anyone can challenge a listing on the FCC's map.
  • Only claims of home service count. A building where every claim is business-only drops out. On a few mostly commercial blocks, Verizon claims home service and the cable company claims only business service, so the map shows no cable company there.
  • The map shows blocks. Buildings on the same block, or across the street, can have different options.
  • Verizon's promise counted households, and the FCC counts buildings. The buildings without a fiber claim aren't a count of households still waiting.
  • The franchise map dates from 2016 and names Time Warner and Cablevision. Each block takes the area that holds its center, so a block cut by the line goes to one side.
  • Wireless records don't say what technology they use. The block file lists them, and the map never counts them as a wire.
  • A blank is never a zero. A block with no reported buildings, or very few for its size, is shown in gray.
